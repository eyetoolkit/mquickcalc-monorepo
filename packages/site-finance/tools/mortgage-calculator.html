The real cost of a mortgage

A $400,000 30-year mortgage at 6.5% costs about $2,528/month PITI, but you pay back $910,000 over 30 years — $510,000 of that is interest, more than the original loan. A 15-year term at the same rate cuts the total interest to $260,000, but the monthly payment is $3,484. The 30-year is more affordable monthly; the 15-year is dramatically cheaper in total.

How this calculator works

PITI = Principal & Interest + monthly property tax + monthly insurance. Principal & Interest is calculated with the standard amortization formula. Property tax is (home price × rate) / 12; insurance is (annual premium) / 12.

Frequently asked questions

How much house can I afford on my salary?

The most common rule is the 28/36 rule: your total housing costs (principal, interest, property tax, insurance, and HOA) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total debt payments (housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards, etc.) should not exceed 36%. So if you earn $10,000/month gross, your maximum housing budget is approximately $2,800/month PITI. However, lenders also factor in your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, down payment size, and employment history. Pre-qualification from a lender gives you a more precise number than the rule-of-thumb calculation.

Should I put 20% down to avoid PMI?

Generally yes — PMI (private mortgage insurance) costs 0.5–1.5% of the loan balance per year and protects the lender, not you, if you default. On a $400,000 home with a $320,000 mortgage, PMI at 0.8% adds $2,560/year ($213/month) to your cost. Putting 20% down eliminates this cost entirely. However, if you do not have enough cash for a 20% down payment, putting 10% down with PMI is still better than waiting — the monthly PMI cost is often less than the additional rent you would pay while saving. Once you reach 20% equity (either through payments or home price appreciation), you can request PMI removal.

What is PMI and how do I avoid it?

Private mortgage insurance protects the lender — not you — if you stop making payments and the home goes to foreclosure. It is typically required when your down payment is less than 20% of the home's purchase price. On a conventional loan, you can request PMI removal once your loan-to-value ratio drops below 80% (typically after 5–7 years of payments on a 30-year mortgage, depending on home appreciation). Federal Housing Administration (FHA) loans carry MIP (mortgage insurance premium) that works differently — MIP cannot be removed without refinancing into a conventional loan once you reach 20% equity.

What is the difference between a 15-year and 30-year mortgage?

A 15-year mortgage has dramatically higher monthly payments but saves enormous amounts of total interest. On a $400,000 loan at 6.5%: a 30-year term costs $2,528/month and $509,000 in total interest over 30 years; a 15-year term costs $3,477/month but only $226,000 in total interest — saving $283,000 in interest while paying off 15 years earlier. The 15-year is the better financial decision if you can comfortably afford the higher payment. If the 15-year payment would strain your budget or cause you to skip retirement contributions, the 30-year with extra principal payments is a reasonable alternative — you get flexibility without sacrificing the interest savings of a shorter term.

How does the Fed affect mortgage rates?

Federal Reserve monetary policy influences mortgage rates but does not directly set them. When the Fed raises its benchmark interest rate, mortgage rates typically rise 1–3 months later as lenders adjust to the changed cost of capital. Mortgage rates also reflect expectations for future inflation — if markets expect higher inflation, long-term mortgage rates (which are fixed for 30 years) will be higher to compensate lenders for losing purchasing power over time. The Fed's actions and language can move mortgage rates by 0.25–0.50% within days. When the Fed cuts rates, mortgage rates tend to fall gradually rather than immediately, because lenders also factor in the economic outlook and their own cost of funds.

Should I pay points to lower my mortgage rate?

Mortgage points (also called discount points) are upfront fees paid at closing to reduce the interest rate — each point costs 1% of the loan amount and typically reduces the rate by 0.25%. On a $320,000 loan, one point costs $3,200 and might reduce the rate from 6.5% to 6.25%. Whether paying points makes sense depends on how long you keep the mortgage: if you keep it for 7 years, the $3,200 upfront cost is roughly break-even against the monthly savings. If you sell or refinance within 5 years, paying points is a net loss. For most buyers, taking the lender's par rate (no points) is the right choice unless you are certain you will stay in the home for 10+ years.

What is an escrow account and do I need one?

An escrow account (or impound account) is a separate account where your lender collects money each month as part of your mortgage payment to cover property taxes and homeowners insurance when they come due. Lenders typically require escrow accounts when your down payment is less than 20%, and they may allow (or require) it even with larger down payments. The lender manages the account and pays your tax and insurance bills on your behalf when due. The main benefit is that you avoid a large lump-sum bill twice a year; the downside is that you pay 1/12 of the annual cost each month and can face escrow shortages if taxes or insurance costs rise unexpectedly.
