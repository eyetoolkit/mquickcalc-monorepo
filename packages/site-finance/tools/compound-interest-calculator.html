How compounding frequency matters

A 5% rate compounded daily yields 5.1267% APY; compounded monthly it is 5.116%; annually, 5.0%. The difference is small at low rates, but at 20% or higher, daily compounding can add 0.5-1% extra per year. For practical purposes, monthly is the right default for most savings accounts.

How this calculator works

Compound interest is the financial concept that makes money grow faster over time — you earn interest not only on your initial deposit, but also on the interest that accumulates each period. The formula is: Final = Principal × (1 + rate/n)^(n × years), where n is the number of compounding periods per year. This is the same formula used by banks, bond issuers, and investment platforms to calculate the growth of any fixed-rate investment. The effective Annual Percentage Yield (APY) accounts for compounding within the year and is the figure you should compare when shopping for savings accounts or CDs, not the nominal advertised rate.

To see the actual difference compounding frequency makes: at 5% annual rate, monthly compounding yields (1 + 0.05/12)^12 − 1 = 5.116% APY; daily compounding yields (1 + 0.05/365)^365 − 1 = 5.127% APY. The difference is tiny at low rates — less than 0.02 percentage points. At 20% (some high-yield bonds or aggressive investments), monthly compounding gives 21.94% APY while daily gives 22.13% APY — a more meaningful 0.19 percentage point gap. The lesson: for low-rate savings products, compounding frequency is nearly irrelevant; what matters most is the advertised rate itself.

The power of time in compound interest

The most counterintuitive aspect of compound interest is how dramatically time amplifies its effect. At 7% annual return (roughly the long-term US stock market average), $10,000 becomes $20,700 in 10 years, $42,900 in 20 years, and $76,900 in 30 years — without adding a single dollar. This is why financial advisors consistently say that starting at 25 rather than 35 to retire comfortably is worth millions, even if the monthly contribution is the same. The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate doubling time: divide 72 by the interest rate. At 7%, 72 ÷ 7 = 10.3 years to double. At 4%, 72 ÷ 4 = 18 years.

But compound interest works against you too. Credit card debt compounds against you at rates of 15–25% APR, which means a $5,000 balance with 20% APR will grow to $12,000 in under 5 years if only minimum payments are made — more than doubling the original debt. This is why always paying more than the minimum on credit cards is one of the highest-return financial moves most people can make. The mathematics of compound interest do not care whether it is working for you or against you.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Rule of 72?

A quick mental math shortcut: 72 ÷ interest rate = years to double your money. At 7% (stock market historical average), your money doubles every ~10.3 years. At 4% (high-yield savings account), it takes 18 years. At 2% (regular checking account), it takes 36 years. The rule is mathematically exact for an 8% rate and reasonably accurate for rates between 4% and 12%. Below 4% it slightly underestimates the time needed; above 12% it slightly overestimates. Use it for quick financial estimates, not precise calculations.

How often do real savings accounts compound?

Most US high-yield savings accounts (HYSAs) advertise "daily compounding" but credit interest monthly — meaning interest accrues every day but the actual deposit to your account happens once a month. This is not meaningfully different from monthly compounding for practical purposes. Certificates of Deposit (CDs) typically compound semi-annually. US Treasury bills compound semi-annually. Bond funds and ETFs that reinvest dividends typically compound monthly or quarterly. For stocks and ETFs held long-term, the "compounding" is the reinvested dividends growing the position — the frequency depends on the dividend schedule of the specific holdings.

Why does more frequent compounding only help a little?

Because compound interest's power comes from time, not from compounding frequency. The difference between daily and monthly compounding at 5% interest on $10,000 over 20 years is approximately $127 — about 0.4% of the final amount. At 7%, the gap widens to about $300. At 15%, it becomes roughly $1,200. At 25%, it is approximately $4,500. So for credit card rates (15-25%), compounding frequency matters much more. For savings accounts (under 5%), it barely matters. Always check the APY (effective annual yield), not the nominal rate, when comparing financial products.

What is the difference between APY and APR?

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the nominal interest rate — the stated percentage you earn or owe per year before accounting for compounding within the year. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the effective rate after compounding is factored in. For savings accounts, APY is what you actually earn. For loans and credit cards, lenders are required to disclose APR, but the actual cost of revolving credit is better expressed as APY — which can be significantly higher than APR when interest accrues daily on an outstanding balance. When comparing loans, always compare APY to APY, not APR to APY.

Does compound interest work in reverse for debt?

Yes — and this is the most financially damaging application of compound interest. A $5,000 credit card balance at 22% APR, paid with only the minimum payment (typically 2% of balance or $25, whichever is greater), will take over 20 years to pay off and cost more than $7,000 in total interest paid. The interest each month is charged on the remaining balance, not the original amount — which is exactly the compound interest formula working in reverse. This is why the single most impactful debt payoff strategy is paying more than the minimum, even small additional payments dramatically reduce total interest paid and time to debt freedom.

What rate of return should I use for long-term stock market estimates?

The S&P 500's historical inflation-adjusted annual return is approximately 7% in real terms (after inflation) since 1926. Nominal returns average about 10%. Use 7% for inflation-adjusted estimates and 8-10% for nominal estimates when modeling long-term portfolio growth. No individual stock or cryptocurrency should be modeled at these rates — these are broad market averages. For bonds, use the actual yield to maturity rather than historical averages, as bond returns are much more predictable. For cash savings, use the actual APY being offered, typically 3-5% in 2024-2025 environments.

What about taxes on compound interest?

Interest earned on savings accounts, CDs, and bonds is taxed as ordinary income in the US in the year it is earned, not when it is withdrawn. This means you owe income tax on "phantom gains" — interest that is calculated as earned but not yet in your pocket. In a taxable brokerage account, dividends and capital gains also create tax events, though qualified dividends and long-term capital gains are taxed at lower rates than ordinary income. This is one reason tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA) are so powerful for long-term compounding — they eliminate or defer this tax drag, allowing the full stated return to compound.
