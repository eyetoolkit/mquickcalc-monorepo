How compound interest works

Compound interest means you earn interest on the interest you already earned. A savings account at 5% APY grows about 5% a year on average, but the actual curve steepens over time — most of the growth happens in the back half of the period.

How this calculator works

The calculator uses monthly compounding, which is the standard for high-yield savings accounts. Each month, your balance earns (rate / 12) interest, then your monthly contribution is added on top.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I save each month?

A common guideline is to save 10-15% of your take-home pay for retirement, plus a separate emergency fund of 3-6 months of expenses. Whether you hit that target matters less than consistency — even $50/month over 30 years grows to over $60,000 with a 5% average annual return. If 10-15% feels out of reach, start with whatever you can manage (even 1-2%) and increase by 1% every few months. The psychological habit of saving regularly is more valuable than the specific percentage.

What interest rate should I use?

For a high-yield savings account (HYSA) in 2024-2026, 4-4.5% APY was common. For a standard checking account it is often 0-0.5%. For a CD or Treasury bill it can be 4-5%. For a stock index fund portfolio, the long-term nominal return is about 10% annually (7% after inflation), but year-to-year it varies dramatically — this tool is designed for fixed-rate planning, not stock market projections. If you are modeling a mixed portfolio, use the weighted average expected return of your asset allocation.

Why does the balance grow so much in later years?

Because of compounding: the interest you earn in year 10 is itself earning interest in year 11, and so on. A 5% return on $10,000 is $500 in year 1 but $1,500+ in year 30, because the base has grown to $30,000+. This is why starting early matters more than the amount — someone who invests $200/month for 10 years and then stops will have more at age 65 than someone who invests $200/month for 30 years starting at age 35, because the first 10 years of compounding outpace the later 30.

What is the difference between APY and APR?

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the simple interest rate — the stated rate on your account or loan. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) accounts for compounding and is always higher than APR when interest is compounded more than once per year. For savings accounts, banks advertise APY because it looks better to consumers. For loans, lenders quote APR because it includes fees and looks lower than the true cost when compounded frequently. Always compare APY for savings products and APR for loan products, and make sure you are comparing like with like.

How much do fees reduce my actual return?

Fees have an outsized effect on long-term returns because they compound against you. A 1% annual fee on a $10,000 investment growing at 7% nominal reduces the final value after 30 years from approximately $76,000 to approximately $57,000 — a $19,000 reduction from a 1% fee. This is why index funds with expense ratios of 0.03-0.10% consistently outperform actively managed funds with 0.5-1.5% expense ratios over 20+ year periods. Always check the fee load before committing to any savings or investment product.

Should I pay off debt first or start saving?

Generally, the priority order is: (1) build a small emergency fund ($1,000-2,000) to avoid credit card debt for unexpected expenses, (2) capture any employer 401(k) match (this is an immediate 50-100% return on your money), (3) pay off high-interest debt (anything above your expected investment return), (4) fully fund your emergency fund (3-6 months of expenses), (5) max out tax-advantaged retirement accounts (IRA, 401k). The exception is debt with very low interest (below 4-5%) where the math may favor investing over paying off the debt early.

How does inflation affect my savings?

Inflation erodes the purchasing power of your savings — money saved at 3% inflation loses approximately half its real value over 24 years. At 3% annual inflation, $100 today buys less than $50 worth of goods in 24 years. This is why holding too much of your portfolio in cash or low-yield savings accounts is a long-term wealth eroder. For long-term goals (retirement, a house down payment in 10+ years), at least some of your savings should be in investments that historically outpace inflation — stocks return approximately 7% nominal above inflation over long periods.
