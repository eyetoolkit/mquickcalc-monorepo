How ovulation is estimated

Ovulation is the release of a mature egg from the ovary, which typically occurs approximately 14 days before the start of your next period — this luteal phase length is relatively consistent (10-16 days) compared to the follicular phase which varies more from cycle to cycle. The fertile window begins approximately 5 days before ovulation and ends on the day of ovulation itself, because sperm can survive inside the female reproductive tract for up to 5 days, while the egg is viable for only 12-24 hours after release. The calculator uses your last menstrual period (LMP) and average cycle length to estimate when ovulation occurs, then displays your estimated ovulation date and fertile window. This method works best for women with regular cycles between 21 and 35 days. Women with irregular cycles should use additional tracking methods for more precise ovulation identification.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the ovulation estimate?

The calculator provides an estimate based on cycle averages — it assumes ovulation occurs 14 days before your next period, which is the population average. In reality, only about 30% of women have ovulation at exactly day 14 of a 28-day cycle. Cycle length varies from woman to woman and from month to month, and stress, illness, travel, or changes in exercise can shift ovulation by several days. Use this estimate as a planning guide, not a precise prediction. For women trying to conceive, tracking basal body temperature (BBT) and cervical mucus provides more precise confirmation of ovulation after it occurs.

What if my cycle is irregular?

If your cycles vary by more than 7-10 days from month to month, calendar-based estimation is unreliable and you should use additional tracking methods. Ovulation predictor kits (OPKs) detect the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge that occurs 24-36 hours before ovulation, giving you 1-2 days of advance notice. Basal body temperature tracking (BBT) requires daily morning temperature measurement before getting out of bed — ovulation causes a measurable temperature rise of 0.3-0.5°C that confirms ovulation occurred the previous day. Cervical mucus tracking (the Billings method) identifies the ovulation period by observing changes in discharge consistency throughout the cycle.

When is the best time to try to conceive?

The highest probability of conception is during the 2 days leading up to ovulation and the day of ovulation itself. Having intercourse every other day during the fertile window covers this period without requiring daily intercourse (which can reduce sperm concentration in some men). Sperm can survive for 3-5 days inside the female reproductive tract, so intercourse 2-3 days before ovulation can still result in pregnancy. The probability of conception on any given day during the fertile window is approximately 20-30% for healthy couples under 35 — not 100%, which is a common misconception. Most couples (approximately 85%) conceive within 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.

What are the signs that I am ovulating?

Several physical signs accompany ovulation: a brief ache or twinge on one side of the lower abdomen (mittelschmerz) occurs in approximately 20% of women; a shift in cervical mucus from thick and cloudy to clear, slippery, and stretchy (like raw egg white) indicates approaching ovulation; a slight rise in basal body temperature of 0.3-0.5°C occurs the day after ovulation; some women experience increased libido, breast tenderness, or light spotting. None of these signs is perfectly reliable on its own, which is why combining tracking methods (OPK + BBT + cervical mucus) gives the most accurate picture of your ovulation pattern.

Can I get pregnant outside my fertile window?

Outside the fertile window, the probability of conception is very low — essentially zero on days more than 5 days before ovulation or more than 24 hours after ovulation. However, because cycle length and ovulation timing vary, relying on calendar-based "safe periods" as birth control (the rhythm method) has a typical-use failure rate of approximately 24% per year, making it one of the least reliable contraceptive methods. If you are trying to avoid pregnancy, use a proven method such as condoms, hormonal birth control, or an IUD rather than relying on cycle tracking alone.

How long should I try before seeing a doctor?

For women under 35, consult a fertility specialist (reproductive endocrinologist) after 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse without pregnancy. For women 35 and older, seek evaluation after 6 months because female fertility declines more rapidly after age 35. Earlier evaluation is also warranted if you have known conditions affecting fertility such as PCOS, endometriosis, irregular periods, history of pelvic inflammatory disease, or if your male partner has known fertility issues. A basic fertility workup includes hormone testing (FSH, LH, estradiol, prolactin, thyroid), antral follicle count (via ultrasound), and a semen analysis for the male partner.

What is luteal phase defect and does it affect fertility?

The luteal phase is the time between ovulation and the start of the next period, during which the corpus luteum produces progesterone to support a potential pregnancy. Luteal phase defect (LPD) is a condition where this phase is shorter than 10 days, which may impair implantation because the uterine lining does not have sufficient time to develop and stabilize. LPD is diagnosed by tracking cycles and progesterone blood tests. It is treatable with progesterone supplementation (prescribed by a doctor) if confirmed. A short luteal phase can sometimes be a sign of underlying hormonal issues such as PCOS, thyroid dysfunction, or low ovarian reserve, which warrant broader evaluation.
