How age is calculated

Age calculation sounds simple but involves a few subtle rules. The most common method subtracts the birth year from the current year, then adjusts by whether the birthday has occurred this calendar year. If today is after the birthday date, you are already the full difference in years. If today is before the birthday date, you are one year younger in whole-year terms — because you have not yet completed that trip around the sun.

The algorithm used here computes the age in three steps: first it calculates the raw year difference, then it compares the month and day of the birth date against today's date to decide whether to subtract one. After the year is settled, the same logic applies to months and days: if the current day is before the birth day within the month, the month counter borrows from the previous month and the day counter becomes a positive number. This is why the result always aligns with what you would expect from counting on a calendar — the math follows the calendar structure rather than just doing simple division.

Total days are calculated as the raw difference between the current timestamp and the birth timestamp, divided by the number of milliseconds in a day (86,400,000). This gives the precise elapsed days, which includes the partial day of birth and the partial day of today. For most purposes this is sufficiently accurate. Note that "total days" in this calculation represents the number of midnights crossed — it is the number of 24-hour periods elapsed, not the number of waking hours.

Common use cases for age calculation

Age verification is legally required in many contexts: alcohol and tobacco purchases, gambling and lottery participation, age-restricted content platforms, car rental agreements, and prescription medication purchases all require proof of meeting a minimum age threshold. In the United States, the legal drinking age is 21, not 18 as in most other countries. Many online forms automatically block submission if the calculated age falls below the minimum, using today's date and the entered birthdate to make that determination.

Retirement and benefits eligibility is another common use case. In the United States, full Social Security retirement age is 67 for anyone born after 1960, while Medicare eligibility begins at 65. Pension plans and 401(k) rules have their own age thresholds. Similar age gates exist in other countries for state pensions, health coverage, and senior discounts — knowing your exact age to the day helps you plan when to claim benefits.

School enrollment cutoff dates create an unusual edge case in age calculation. Most public school systems set a cutoff date (often September 1 in the US, varying in other countries) for determining which grade a child enters. A child born on August 31 may be one of the oldest in their class, while one born on September 1 is one of the youngest. This has been studied extensively: children born in the older cohort within a grade tend to show slightly better academic and athletic performance in early years, though these effects diminish over time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate age in years only?

The fastest way is to subtract your birth year from the current year, then check whether your birthday has already passed this year. If it has not, subtract one more year. For example, if you were born in 1990 and today is before your birthday date in 2025, you are 34. If it is after your birthday date, you are 35. This is the standard method used in most legal and administrative contexts. It does not account for the exact day, which is why forms that ask for age rather than date of birth are considered less reliable for age verification.

Does age calculation include the current day?

No — by standard convention, age increments at the moment of your birthday, not at the start of the day. If today is your birthday, you are one year older than you were yesterday, not still the previous age. Our calculator uses this convention. Some legal contexts use a different rule: in some East Asian countries, everyone is considered age 1 at birth (counting from conception), and everyone increments age on New Year's Day rather than their individual birthday. This is called "East Asian age reckoning" and is largely historical, but some Korean and Chinese contexts may still use it informally.

How does leap year affect age calculation?

Leap years affect age calculation only when the birth date or current date falls on February 29. A person born on February 29 is legally considered to have their birthday on February 28 or March 1 in non-leap years, depending on the jurisdiction. In most legal and administrative systems, the birthday is observed on the last day of February, so a person born on February 29 turns 18 on February 28 in non-leap years. Our calculator follows this convention. The raw day-counting method will still produce a reasonable elapsed-day figure even in leap years because JavaScript's Date object handles February 29 correctly.

What is the Korean age system?

South Korea traditionally used "East Asian age" (nolja), where everyone is considered age 1 at birth and everyone increments age on January 1. Under this system, two people born on January 1 and December 31 of the same year would both be age 2 on February 1 of the following year — even though one is only two months old. South Korea officially switched to the international age system (called "man-nai" — "full age") in June 2023, meaning that Korean official and legal contexts now use the same age calculation as the rest of the world. However, the traditional Korean age system is still widely used in casual social contexts, so a Korean person may give you a different age than what a Western calculator shows.

Can I use this for pet age in human years?

No — dogs and cats age differently from humans, and the common "multiply by 7" rule is a rough approximation that overestimates age in young animals and underestimates it in older ones. Dogs age rapidly in their first two years (a 1-year-old dog is roughly equivalent to a 15-year-old human, not a 7-year-old), and the rate of aging slows after that. The more accurate formula is: human years = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31, for dogs older than 1 year. Use our dog age calculator or cat age calculator for pet-specific conversions.

Why might my calculated age differ from official records?

In most cases, the difference comes from which day boundary is used for the calculation. Some systems calculate age based on whether the birthday has been reached by the start of the current day (midnight), while others use the current moment within the day. Our calculator uses the current time of day, which is more precise. A more significant discrepancy can occur if your birth date was recorded in a different calendar system — for example, if you were born in a country that used the Julian calendar before switching to the Gregorian calendar, or if your birth date was entered with an error. Always double-check the birth date field if you see an unexpected result.

Is age calculated differently in different countries?

The mathematical concept of age is universal, but how it is applied in practice varies. In most countries, you turn a specific age on your birthday. In some European countries, a child starts school on the year they turn 6, regardless of their birth month — so two children born in January and December of the same year would start in the same cohort. In the United States, school cutoff dates mean that children born after the cutoff are often among the youngest in their class. In Japan, the legal age of adulthood was 20 until 2022, when it was lowered to 18. Knowing how your specific jurisdiction handles age for the purpose you need — legal, financial, educational — is always more reliable than assuming the calculation is universal.
